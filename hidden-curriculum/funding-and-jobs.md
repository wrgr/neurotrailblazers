| **You need** | The name of one PI, and ideally one grant number from a paper's acknowledgements |

[Career mechanics]({{ '/hidden-curriculum/career-mechanics/' | relative_url }})
explains the shape of the machine and prints no numbers, because most of them change
yearly. This page is the companion that prints the ones written down somewhere
official, with the source beside each. Everything here was read from an agency page or
a primary report on the date given. Where a fact could not be reached, it is absent
rather than approximated.

Two things you will be asked to do without instruction: work out how you are being paid
and what that implies, and work out who funds a lab before you write to it. Both are
answerable in under an hour from public records, once you know which record and which
words.

## 1. Four ways a trainee is paid, and what each one says about your status

The label on your paycheck encodes who decided to pay you, for how long, and whether
the money follows you.

| Route | Who holds the award | Who chose you | Ends when |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Research assistantship** | The PI, on a project grant (R01, U01, UM1) | The PI | The budget line or the project does |
| **Teaching assistantship** | The department | The department, against teaching need | The teaching need or the appointment term does |
| **Individual fellowship** | You, through your institution (NIH F31/F32, NSF GRFP) | A review panel, on your application | The fellowship's term does, wherever you are |
| **Training-grant appointment** | The institution, on a training grant (T32) | The training grant's PI | Your appointment term does |

NIH states the distinction in its own words: individual fellowships "provide research
training opportunities to help prepare fellows for careers in biomedical, behavioral,
social, and clinical research"; institutional training programs "provide institutions
with funds to develop or enhance research training opportunities for individuals,
selected by the institution"; career development awards "provide protected time for
additional mentored or non-mentored experiences in a productive scientific environment"
([NIH, Research Training and Career Development](https://grants.nih.gov/funding/funding-categories/research-training-and-career-development),
checked 27 September 2026). The phrase that matters is *selected by the institution*: on
a T32 you were chosen by a PI running a program, not by a national panel, and the
appointment is a term, not an award of your own.

**What each route means for you.**

- **Research assistantship.** You are a line in someone else's budget. The end date is
  the project's, and §4 shows how to read it in the public record.
- **Teaching assistantship.** Tied to a course, not a project. It does not end when a
  grant ends. The cost is time that is not research time; the benefit is independence
  from one lab's finances.
- **Individual fellowship.** Reviewed against you, and it moves with you. On the current
  NIH parent announcements an F31 "may receive up to 5 years of aggregate
  Kirschstein-NRSA support at the predoctoral level"
  ([PA-25-422](https://www.grants.nih.gov/grants/guide/pa-files/PA-25-422.html)) and an
  F32 "up to 3 years of aggregate Kirschstein-NRSA support at the postdoctoral level"
  ([PA-25-423](https://grants.nih.gov/grants/guide/pa-files/PA-25-423.html)). Both
  require that "by the time of award" you be "a citizen or a non-citizen national of the
  United States or have been lawfully admitted for permanent residence." That sentence
  closes the NIH fellowship route to most international trainees (§8).
- **Training-grant appointment.** "Training grants offset the cost of stipends, tuition
  and fees, and training related expenses, including health insurance, for the
  appointed trainees." There is an obligation attached: "The NRSA legislation requires
  some recipients of support (post-doctoral fellows and trainees) to pay back the
  Federal government by engaging in health-related research, health-related research
  training or health-related teaching"
  ([NIH, Institutional Training](https://grants.nih.gov/funding/funding-categories/research-training-and-career-development/institutional-training)).
  Ask what the payback terms are before you accept a postdoctoral appointment.

**The stipend figure, with its source.** NIH sets NRSA stipends once a year in a
numbered notice. The current one, NOT-OD-26-044, released 18 March 2026 and "effective
with all Kirschstein-NRSA awards made on or after October 1, 2025," sets the
predoctoral stipend at $29,364 and postdoctoral stipends from $63,480 (0 years of
experience) through $63,900 (1 year) to $77,076 (7 or more years), with
training-related expenses of $4,750 per predoctoral and $12,400 per postdoctoral
trainee
([NOT-OD-26-044](https://www.grants.nih.gov/grants/guide/notice-files/NOT-OD-26-044.html),
checked 27 September 2026). Those figures bind NRSA fellows and trainees. The notice
does not set pay for a research assistantship on an R01; your institution does, so the
notice is a reference point, not your salary.

**How common each route is.** The Survey of Earned Doctorates is "an annual census of
research doctorate recipients from U.S. academic institutions." In the 2025 tables,
released 25 September 2026, of 9,059 biological and biomedical sciences recipients
reporting a primary source of support, 40.4% named research assistantships or
traineeships, 35.3% fellowships, scholarships, or dissertation grants, 12.4% teaching
assistantships, and 9.6% their own resources; across all fields, 36.8%, 24.0%, 20.2%,
and 15.2%
([NSF 26-326, Table 4-1](https://ncses.nsf.gov/pubs/nsf26326/assets/data-tables/tables/nsf26326-tab004-001.pdf)).
The table's footnote says the first category "includes research assistantships, other
assistantships, traineeships, internships, or clinical residencies," so it does not
separate an R01-paid RA from a T32 appointee. And roughly one biology doctorate in ten
was paid for mainly from the recipient's own pocket. If that is you, it is not unusual,
and it is the strongest reason on this page to apply for the portable money in §7.

## 2. The codes: what F31, F32, T32, K99/R00, R01, U01, and UM1 mean for you

An NIH **activity code** is "a 3-character code used to identify a specific category of
extramural research activity"
([NIH Grants Policy Statement, 1.2](https://grants.nih.gov/grants/policy/nihgps/html5/section_1/1.2_definition_of_terms.htm)).
You will hear the codes as nouns: "she's on an F32," "the R01 is up for renewal," "it's
a UM1, so there's a steering committee." Each tells you who holds the money and where
you sit relative to it. The descriptions are NIH's own
([activity code list](https://grants.nih.gov/grants/funding/ac_search_results.htm) and
the [UM1 page](https://grants.nih.gov/funding/activity-codes/UM1), checked 27 September
2026).

| Code | NIH's description | Who holds it | What it means for your status |
|---|---|---|---|
| **F31** | "To provide predoctoral individuals with supervised research training in specified health and health-related areas leading toward the research degree (e.g., Ph.D.)." | You, as a predoctoral fellow | Your own award. You are the applicant, with a sponsor. Portable within its rules. |
| **F32** | "To provide postdoctoral research training to individuals to broaden their scientific background and extend their potential for research in specified health-related areas." | You, as a postdoctoral fellow | Same, at the postdoctoral level. |
| **T32** | "To enable institutions to make National Research Service Awards to individuals selected by them for predoctoral and postdoctoral research training in specified shortage areas." | The institution; a program PI | You are appointed to a slot for a term. The award is not yours. |
| **K99** | "To support the initial phase of a Career/Research Transition award program that provides 1-2 years of mentored support for highly motivated, advanced postdoctoral research scientists." | You, in a mentor's lab | Your own award, and a signal to hiring committees that the second phase comes with you. |
| **R00** | "To support the second phase of a Career/Research Transition award program that provides 1 -3 years of independent research support (R00) contingent on securing an independent research position." | You, as a new independent investigator | Activates only once you hold an independent position. |
| **R01** | "To support a discrete, specified, circumscribed project to be performed by the named investigator(s) in an area representing his or her specific interest and competencies." | The PI, through the institution | You are staff on the project. The end date is the project's. |
| **U01** | Same description as the R01, under the title "Research Project--Cooperative Agreements." | The PI, with NIH involvement | As R01, plus NIH program staff in the room. |
| **UM1** | "To support cooperative agreements involving large-scale research activities with complicated structures, e.g. clinical networks, research programs or consortium." | A center or consortium PI | You are staff in a multi-lab structure with its own governance. |

**What "cooperative agreement" means, in the funder's words.** The BRAIN CONNECTS
mouse-centers announcement, the one this site's home project was funded under, says the
instrument is "an 'assistance' mechanism (rather than an 'acquisition' mechanism), in
which substantial NIH programmatic involvement with the recipients is anticipated," and
that "the NIH's purpose is to support and stimulate the recipients' activities by
involvement in and otherwise working jointly with the award recipients in a partnership
role; it is not to assume direction, prime responsibility, or a dominant role"
([RFA-NS-22-048](https://grants.nih.gov/grants/guide/rfa-files/RFA-NS-22-048.html)).
On a U01 or UM1, decisions about scope and data release can come from a steering
committee your PI sits on rather than from your PI alone. When a priority changes and
nobody in the lab seems to have chosen it, this is usually why.

**K99/R00, as the current NIH page states it.** Purpose: "to increase and maintain a
strong cohort of new and talented, NIH-supported, independent investigators."
Eligibility: "U.S. citizen or non-citizen, with research or clinical doctoral degree,
and no more than 4 years of Post-Doctoral research experience," and "PD/PI U.S.
Citizenship Requirement: No"
([K99 activity code page](https://grants.nih.gov/funding/activity-codes/K99), checked
27 September 2026). The limit counts postdoctoral research experience, not time in
your current lab, so years spent in an earlier postdoc count against it; the eligibility
section of the announcement you apply under gives the exact rule. And the citizenship line is the reverse of the F
awards: this is the NIH transition award open to international postdocs.

**Diversity-focused variants, as far as the current pages go.** NIGMS describes MOSAIC
as "an NIH-wide initiative that NIGMS oversees" with "two components: a postdoctoral
career transition award (K99/R00) and a cohort-based mentoring and career development
program that supports the scholars (UE5 research education cooperative agreement),"
which "facilitates the timely transition of individuals from diverse backgrounds from
their mentored postdoctoral research positions to research-intensive faculty positions"
([NIGMS, July 2024](https://nigms.nih.gov/loop/2024/07/new-mosaic-funding-opportunities-and-upcoming-webinar)).
That post lists announcements reissued in 2024; whether one is open today is a fact to
confirm in the NIH Guide, where expired announcements are labeled as such. The same
discipline applies to any diversity-targeted fellowship: read the expiration date
before planning a year around it.

## 3. A grant number is six fields, and each one is a fact about your position

This site's home project is cited as UM1NS132250; in the public record its current
line is 5UM1NS132250-04. NIH's Office of Extramural Research publishes a one-page key,
"Deciphering NIH Application/Grant Numbers," with the example 1 R01 CA 654321 01 A1
([era.nih.gov](https://era.nih.gov/files/Deciphering_NIH_Application.pdf), checked
27 September 2026). Read the number left to right:

| Field | In 5UM1NS132250-04 | What NIH says it is |
|---|---|---|
| Application type | 5 | "5=Non-competing Continuation: Request or award for a subsequent budget period within a previously approved project for which a recipient does not have to compete with other applications." A 1 is "Request for support of a project that has not yet been funded"; a 2 is a renewal. |
| Activity code | UM1 | The mechanism (§2). |
| Institute code | NS | NINDS, from the key's own table: the institute that administers the award. |
| Serial number | 132250 | "Six-digit number assigned within an Institute/Center." |
| Support year | 04 | "Two-digit number indicating segment or budget period of a project." |
| Suffix | (none) | "'A' and related number identifies the amendment number (e.g. A1 = resubmission); 'S' and related number identifies the revision record." |

So 5UM1NS132250-04 is the fourth budget period of a cooperative-agreement center
administered by NINDS, and 1UM1NS132250-01, the first row in the record, was the new
award. Papers cite activity code plus institute plus serial, UM1NS132250, which is the
form this site uses.

**What the number tells you and what it does not.** It tells you the mechanism, the
institute, and how far into the project period you are. The mouse-centers RFA states
"The maximum project period is 5 years," and the record's project end date is
31 August 2028, so support year 04 means one more budget period remains under this
award unless something new is submitted. It does not tell you whether your
position is charged to it, or whether a renewal is being written. Those are the two
questions in the first script below.

**Opportunity numbers follow a stated pattern too.** NIH's example: "RFA Numbering
(e.g., RFA-HL-24-004): Indicates an RFA issued by NHLBI (HL) in 2024 or for funding in
2024 (24) with an associated serial number (004)"
([Understand Funding Opportunities](https://grants.nih.gov/grants-process/plan-to-apply/find-your-opportunity-contacts-and-due-dates/understand-funding-opportunities)).
By that pattern RFA-NS-22-048, the opportunity behind UM1NS132250, is a NINDS request
from 2022, serial 048. An RFA is a targeted call, and this one had a single due date
(§5); a Parent Announcement is a "broad funding opportunit[y] allowing applicants to
submit an investigator-initiated application," taking applications on the standard dates
in §5 until its expiration date.

## 4. Finding who funds a lab takes ten minutes, and here is the record to read

RePORTER "is an electronic tool that allows users to search a repository of NIH-funded
research projects," and "in general, the RePORTER database of research projects is
updated weekly" ([RePORT FAQ](https://report.nih.gov/faqs), checked 27 September 2026).

1. **Get a handle.** The PI's surname, the institution, or a grant number from the
   funding statement of a recent paper. The number is best.
2. **Open [reporter.nih.gov](https://reporter.nih.gov/)** and search by it. With a grant
   number, use the core form (UM1NS132250), not one year's line.
3. **Open the project record.** For UM1NS132250 the
   [record](https://reporter.nih.gov/project-details/UM1NS132250) shows the title,
   "BRAIN CONNECTS: A Center for High-throughput Integrative Mouse Connectomics"; the
   contact PI, Jeff W. Lichtman; the administering institute, NINDS; the project start
   and end, 8 September 2023 to 31 August 2028; the opportunity, RFA-NS-22-048; one row
   per fiscal year, 1UM1NS132250-01 (FY2023, $14,497,158), 5UM1NS132250-02 (FY2024,
   $3,335,651), -03 (FY2025, $3,656,998), and -04 (FY2026, $3,377,975), as read on
   27 September 2026; and the abstract.
4. **Read three things.** The **project end date**, the outer bound of any position paid
   from this award. The **type code on the latest row**: a 5 means the project is inside
   its approved period; a new 1 or 2 means a competition happened. The **abstract's
   commitments**, which are what the PI promised the funder, and read them on the
   latest row, because the text can change between years. On this record the FY2025
   and FY2026 abstracts say "we will involve high school, undergraduate and graduate
   students in the proofreading and scientific discovery phases of our work, offering
   trainees mentoring as well as research experience"; the FY2023 and FY2024 abstracts
   worded the same commitment around "undergraduates from underrepresented backgrounds."
   The science is discussed in the
   [HI-MC case study]({{ '/content-library/case-studies/mouseconnects-himc/' | relative_url }}).
5. **Count the rows.** Four fiscal-year rows on a five-year maximum means one budget
   period remains under this award. The first-year amount differs from the later ones;
   the record gives numbers, not reasons, and the reason is a question for the lab
   rather than a guess.
6. **Note what is missing.** RePORTER covers NIH. Support from NSF, foundations,
   industry, or the institution's own funds does not appear, and neither does which
   person is charged to which award. What you find is a floor; the first script turns it
   into an answer. For a non-NIH lab the pattern is the same: the funder's public award
   record, the end date, the abstract, then the question.

## 5. Positions open on the funder's calendar, and the calendar is public

NIH runs three cycles a year. Standard due dates for new applications: February 5,
June 5, October 5 for R01s; April 8, August 8, December 8 for fellowships; February 12,
June 12, October 12 for K awards; January 25, May 25, September 25 for T awards
([Standard Due Dates](https://grants.nih.gov/grants/how-to-apply-application-guide/due-dates-and-submission-policies/due-dates.htm),
checked 27 September 2026). The same page gives what happens next:

| | Application due dates | Scientific merit review | Advisory council round | Earliest project start date |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Cycle I** | January 25 – May 7 | June – July | August or October | September or December |
| **Cycle II** | May 25 – September 7 | October – November | January | April |
| **Cycle III** | September 25 – January 7 | February – March | May | July |

NIH adds that "the actual date of the Advisory Council may occur in the month before or
after the month listed." Read off the table, a due date to the earliest possible start
is about six to ten months, and a position paid from a new award cannot begin before the
right-hand column.

- **"If the grant comes through, you'd start after" has a date in it.** Ask which due
  date the application went to, then read across the row. Council is when the decision
  becomes known; the earliest start is when money can exist.
- **Each year is its own award.** A budget period is "the time interval from the start
  date of a funded portion of an award to the end date of that funded portion (usually
  12 months)," and each arrives as a type 5 line with a Notice of Award, "the official,
  legally binding document ... that ... notifies the recipient of the award of a grant"
  ([NIH Grants Policy Statement, 1.2](https://grants.nih.gov/grants/policy/nihgps/html5/section_1/1.2_definition_of_terms.htm)).
  "We're waiting on the NoA" is a real gate, not a stall. A no-cost extension is "an
  extension of time ... without additional Federal funds or competition": the position
  can lengthen without new money to pay for it.
- **Center programs hire in waves on the program's dates.** The mouse-centers RFA had
  one due date, June 13, 2023, and expired the next day; awards started that September;
  and "a fourth FOA supporting a Data Coordinating Center will be released after awards
  from the first FOAs are made"
  ([RFA-NS-22-048](https://grants.nih.gov/grants/guide/rfa-files/RFA-NS-22-048.html)).
  So coordinating-center work could not be funded until after the centers were, and
  positions paid from any of these awards end no later than the awards do. When you see a consortium, find its RFA and its awards'
  end dates before deciding how long the door is open.

Positions open a few months after money exists, money exists a few months after
council, and council is months after a due date that was public all along. Work
backward from the due date.

## 6. Large-scale connectomics created jobs that are not "graduate student" or "postdoc"

A whole-brain reconstruction is run by more kinds of people than a single-lab project,
and most of the roles have no textbook name. The categories below are the ones funders
and consortia describe in their own documents; where this page has no official example,
it says so.

**Proofreading.** FlyWire describes three kinds of proofreader in one project: "The
majority of the proofreading was carried out and orchestrated by the Murthy and Seung
labs at Princeton University, the Jefferis Lab at MRC Laboratory of Molecular Biology
and the Bock lab at University of Vermont"; the consortium "worked with SixEleven and
ariadne.ai for proofreading services"; and "hundreds of members of the FlyWire
Consortium, including citizen scientists" took part
([flywire.ai](https://flywire.ai/), checked 27 September 2026). Lab staff, contractors,
and volunteers did the same task under three employment statuses, so before you accept
"proofreader" as a title, ask which of the three it is. The current HI-MC abstract
commits that project to involving "high school, undergraduate and graduate students in
the proofreading and scientific discovery phases" (§4), so proofreading is also a
documented entry point for trainees. The
[FlyWire case study]({{ '/content-library/case-studies/flywire-whole-brain/' | relative_url }})
shows what the work is.

**Annotation and curation.** In the same project, "the Jefferis and Bock labs curated
hierarchical annotations for all neurons" and "the Murthy and Seung labs created cell
type annotations for all intrinsic neurons." Curation is scientific work done inside
labs, with authorship stakes, so the
[Module 25]({{ '/modules/module25/' | relative_url }}) discipline of saying what you
did versus what you were given applies from day one.

**Data engineering and pipelines.** The mouse-centers RFA requires that "successful
Centers will establish and scale complete pipelines from sample collection through data
integration and dissemination." A pipeline that must run for a five-year project period
is a job for people whose deliverable is the pipeline rather than the paper. The RFA
also promised a separate Data Coordinating Center; the coordination bodies this site
sits beside, IC3 and APEX, are on the
[CONNECTS ecosystem]({{ '/core/connects-ecosystem/' | relative_url }}) page.

**Program and coordination roles.** A cooperative agreement has governance. The RFA
creates a Steering Committee of "the PDs/PIs of awards of the BRAIN CONNECTS FOAs ...
along with one NIH extramural program representative" and assigns NIH "Project
Coordinator(s)" who "will provide technical assistance, advice, coordination, and other
program actions." Those are jobs on the funder's side; who staffs the awardee side is a
question for the center.

**Core facilities and instrument roles.** Not separately sourced here. The pipelines
begin at "sample collection," and someone runs the microscopes, but who, and on what
contract, is in the center's own postings and on no official page this one could read.

Three questions for any posting in this field: which stage of the pipeline the role
sits in; which award pays for it, so you can read its end date (§4); and whether it is a
research role with authorship or a service role with deliverables. Either can be the
right job. Confusing them is how a year of proofreading becomes a CV line that says
"member, consortium," which
[Career mechanics §4]({{ '/hidden-curriculum/career-mechanics/' | relative_url }})
explains how to avoid.

## 7. The application windows that are actually written down

Only dates read from an official page on 27 September 2026. They move; the announcement
is the authority.

- **NIH F31 and F32.** April 8, August 8, December 8. The current parent announcements,
  PA-25-422 and PA-25-423, were posted June 13, 2025 and expire May 8, 2028.
- **NIH K awards, including K99/R00.** February 12, June 12, October 12.
- **NIH T32.** January 25, May 25, September 25, but the applicant is the institution.
  Your route onto a T32 is the program PI, and the question is whether a slot is open
  this year.
- **NSF Graduate Research Fellowship.** "$37,000 stipend plus $16,000 cost of education
  (COE) paid to the institution for each year of funding support," for "three years
  over a five-year period"; Life Sciences deadline "October 19, 2026 by 8:00 p.m.
  Eastern time" ([NSF GRFP](https://www.nsf.gov/funding/initiatives/grfp)).
  Eligibility: "a U.S. citizen, national, or a permanent resident," and graduate
  students "can apply only once, in the first year of their first graduate program,"
  having "completed less than one academic year in a graduate degree program"
  ([eligibility](https://www.nsf.gov/funding/initiatives/grfp/eligibility)). If you are
  a first-year, the window is this October and does not reopen.
- **HHMI Hanna H. Gray Fellows.** "No more than 7 years of postdoctoral training
  experience by December 15, 2026"
  ([HHMI](https://www.hhmi.org/programs/hanna-h-gray-fellows)).
- **Wellcome Discovery Awards.** Location eligibility changes on 29 October 2026 (§8)
  ([Wellcome](https://wellcome.org/grant-funding/schemes/discovery-awards)).

The GRFP window for a graduate student is the first year of the doctorate, which is
exactly when nobody tells you to apply. The rule from
[Career mechanics §6]({{ '/hidden-curriculum/career-mechanics/' | relative_url }})
holds: the money deadline is earlier than you think.

## 8. Outside the United States, the eligibility paragraph comes before the science

The ERC Starting Grant is for "researchers of any nationality with 0-10 years of
experience since completion of PhD," counted from "the certified date of the successful
PhD defence"; it "may be awarded up to €1.5 million for a period of 5 years," and the
host must be "located in one of the EU Member States or associated countries"
([ERC](https://erc.europa.eu/apply-grant/starting-grant), checked 27 September 2026).
Wellcome's Discovery Awards fund "established researchers and teams," average "£3.5
million," and run "up to 8 years." The administering organisation must currently be in
the UK, the Republic of Ireland, or a low- or middle-income country "(apart from India
and mainland China)," but "from 29 October 2026, we will only accept applications from
lead applicants based at eligible administering organisations in the UK and low- and
middle-income countries in Africa, South Asia and South-East Asia"
([Wellcome](https://wellcome.org/grant-funding/schemes/discovery-awards), checked
28 September 2026). That is the
award a future PI holds, not one you apply for as a trainee. HHMI's Hanna H. Gray
program "supports postdoctoral scientists who show exceptional promise as future
research leaders," allows a fellow "with HHMI approval" to "move to a different
institution in the United States or overseas," and appoints fellows who obtain a
faculty position as Freeman Hrabowski Scholars
([HHMI](https://www.hhmi.org/programs/hanna-h-gray-fellows)).

Set the rules side by side. NIH F awards: citizen, national, or permanent resident. NIH
K99/R00: no citizenship requirement, four-year postdoctoral clock. ERC: any nationality,
EU or associated host. NSF GRFP: citizen, national, or permanent resident, first year
only. Your passport and your host's country together decide which of these exist for
you, and the sentence that says so is near the top of every announcement. Read it before
the science.

## The scripts: questions to ask

Each section above ends in a fact no public record holds. These are the words.

### The script: asking for the grant number

To the PI, when you accept a position or at the start of a year. Not in a crisis.

> "Could you tell me the grant number my position is charged to? I'd like to read the
> RePORTER record so I understand the end date and what the project committed to, and
> then I won't need to ask you the basics."

The moves: you ask for a *number*, a fact the PI can give in one line, rather than for
a conversation about money. *"Then I won't need to ask you the basics"* offers something
in return. With the number, §4 gives you the end date, the type code, and the abstract,
and your next question can be the informed one from
[Career mechanics §1]({{ '/hidden-curriculum/career-mechanics/' | relative_url }}):
is a renewal or a follow-on in preparation.

### The script: asking whether a fellowship changes the answer

To a prospective or current PI, when you are eligible for a portable award.

> "I'm eligible for the F31 [or the GRFP], and I'd like to apply for the [April 8 /
> October] date. If it came through, would that change what you could offer, or the
> timing? I'd rather know now than assume."

The moves: naming the date shows you have read the announcement. *"Would that change
what you could offer"* is the real question, and a PI can answer it honestly, because a
funded trainee costs the lab less. *"I'd rather know now than assume"* releases the PI
from having to promise anything.

### The script: reading a consortium posting

To the person named in a posting, before you apply.

> "Which part of the pipeline does this role sit in, which award pays for it, and is it
> a research position with authorship or a service position with deliverables? Any of
> those answers is fine; I want to describe the work accurately on my CV later."

The moves: three named categories, so a vague answer is visibly vague. *"Which award"*
lets you do §4 yourself. *"Any of those answers is fine"* is true, and it is what makes
the question answerable rather than adversarial.

## Check yourself

<details markdown="1">
<summary>A paper's funding statement says "supported by NIH UM1NS132250." What can you
know in ten minutes, and what can you not?</summary>

Can know, from RePORTER: the mechanism (UM1, a large cooperative agreement with a
steering committee), the institute (NINDS), the contact PI, the project start and end
dates, the opportunity it was funded under, one award row per fiscal year, and the
abstract with its commitments.

Cannot know: whether any particular person's position is charged to it, whether other
money exists in the lab, or whether a renewal is being written. Those need the first
script.
</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>You are an international student in your first year of a US PhD. Which of the
F31, the GRFP, and the K99/R00 are open to you?</summary>

Not the F31: the parent announcement requires "a citizen or a non-citizen national of the
United States or ... lawfully admitted for permanent residence" by the time of award.
Not the GRFP: "a U.S. citizen, national, or a permanent resident." The K99/R00, later:
the NIH page states "PD/PI U.S. Citizenship Requirement: No" and a limit of "no more
than 4 years of Post-Doctoral research experience." And the ERC Starting Grant is for
"researchers of any nationality" if your host is in the EU or an associated country.

So the portable-money advice in §1 applies to you differently, and the time to find out
which non-NIH fellowships your institution or country offers is now, not in year four.
</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Your PI says: "The R01 goes in October 5. If it's funded, you'd start after
that." When is "after that"?</summary>

October 5 falls in Cycle III (September 25 – January 7). Review is February – March,
council is May, and the earliest project start date is July. So "after that" means July
at the earliest, and the decision becomes known around May, with NIH's caution that
council "may occur in the month before or after the month listed."

Put May and July in your calendar now. Your alternatives need to be live by spring, not
by July, because if the answer is no, a resubmission goes to a later due date and starts
down another row of the same table.
</details>

## What this page does not cover

- **Success rates and funding rates.** NIH publishes them in the NIH Data Book, which
  could not be read as text for this page, so no figure is printed. Look them up there
  by mechanism and institute.
- **Salaries for research assistantships and staff roles.** Set by institutions and
  postings, not by any page this one could quote. The NRSA notice in §1 is the only
  official figure, and it binds only NRSA fellows and trainees.
- **How to write a fellowship application.** Your grants office and the announcement's
  own instructions.
- **Immigration and visa rules**, which interact with every eligibility line above.
  Establish them with an institutional adviser early.
