Episode 1 · 4:52 · Published October 12, 2026

What is connectomics?

What brain cells do, why their connections matter, and what a map can and cannot tell us.

Download episode 1 (MP3, 4.7 MB)Download script
Read episode 1 script

Will: Welcome to the NeuroTrailblazers Podcast. I'm Will.

Julian: And I'm Julian. I'm six and a half, and Will is my dad. Dad, what are we figuring out today?

Will: How scientists make maps of brain connections. The name for that science is connectomics.

Julian: Connectomics. That's a lot of word.

Will: It is. Here's an easier place to start. When someone kicks a soccer ball toward you, how do you know where to put your foot?

Julian: I watch the ball. Then I try to stop it. Sometimes it goes right past me.

Will: Your eyes and brain are working together. Messages travel between cells, and your brain sends signals that help your muscles move. That happens very quickly.

Julian: Before I can even say, here comes the ball?

Will: Often, yes. Those message-carrying cells are called neurons. They have long, thin branches, and they connect with other cells.

Julian: Like little paths going everywhere?

Will: That's a useful way to picture it. Connectomics asks where those paths go, which cells they connect, and what we can learn from the pattern.

Julian: So we're making a map of the paths.

Will: Exactly. That map is called a connectome. You don't have to remember every new word. Start with the idea: which cell connects to which other cell?

Julian: How does a message get from one cell to the next?

Will: At many connections, one cell sends a tiny chemical message across a very small gap. The place where the cells communicate is called a synapse.

Julian: Like passing a note across a little space?

Will: A bit like that. No paper notes, of course. But the message can change what the next cell does. Lots of small connections help a group of cells work together.

Julian: Can't you just look at a brain and see the paths?

Will: Most are much too small to see with our eyes. Think about looking at your school from far away. You can see the building, but you can't see all the doors and hallways inside.

Julian: You need to get closer. A lot closer.

Will: Right. Different tools show different things. Some show big routes through a brain. Others show tiny connections between cells. Both kinds of maps can help.

Julian: What could the little connections tell us?

Will: Take seeing movement. Cells at the back of your eye help work out which way something is moving. Studying their connections has helped scientists understand how they do that.

Julian: So, a ball going left instead of right. That would be useful for soccer.

Will: It would. And it gives scientists a question to test. Does this pattern of connections help these cells notice movement in a certain direction?

Julian: Could your map tell you that I'm thinking about scoring a goal?

Will: The map alone couldn't. A street map shows where the roads go. It doesn't show which cars are on them right now. We also need ways to measure what brain cells are doing.

Julian: The map gives you part of the story. You still have to watch what happens.

Will: You've got it. We put different kinds of clues together. A map, the cells' signals, and what an animal does can each teach us something.

Julian: And why does that matter for people who are sick?

Will: We want to understand what changes when a brain is hurt or doesn't work as it should. Better maps may help researchers find useful questions to test. A map itself isn't a treatment.

Julian: So it's a step toward figuring things out. There's still more work to do.

Will: Yes. Here's a question for our listeners. Think about a map of your school or neighborhood. What could it tell you, and what would it leave out? Give yourself a moment.

Julian: My school map could show the lunchroom. But it couldn't tell me what's for lunch. I'd still need to find that out.

Will: Exactly. Our starting point is the Neuronauts story at NeuroTrailblazers dot org. Next time, we'll explain NIH BRAIN CONNECTS, a program helping teams build better brain maps.

Julian: Okay. We learned the science word. Next comes the program. See you next time!

Episode 2 · 5:23 · Published October 12, 2026

What is NIH BRAIN CONNECTS?

How the National Institutes of Health (NIH) BRAIN CONNECTS program supports the tools needed to map brain connections, and how to judge progress.

Read episode 2 script

Will: Welcome to the NeuroTrailblazers Podcast. I'm Will.

Julian: I'm Julian. I'm six and a half. Dad, we talked about connectomics. Now what is NIH BRAIN CONNECTS?

Will: Connectomics is the science of mapping brain connections. NIH BRAIN CONNECTS is a research program that helps teams develop the tools to make those maps.

Julian: So the science and the program have different jobs.

Will: Yes. Think about soccer. Soccer is the game. An organization can bring teams together and help them play. The organization and the game aren't the same thing.

Julian: Okay. And what do the letters NIH mean?

Will: National Institutes of Health. It's part of the United States government, and it supports health research. Its BRAIN Initiative is a larger effort to help scientists understand the brain.

Julian: And BRAIN CONNECTS is one part of that effort. I think I've got it.

Will: Right. A big goal is to make it possible to map connections across entire brains. To get there, teams need tools that can handle far more work, accurately and at a reasonable cost.

Julian: Does that mean someone has already mapped every tiny connection in a whole human brain?

Will: No. That's much bigger than the detailed maps we have now. A goal tells us what people are working toward. It doesn't mean they've already finished it.

Julian: What do the teams need to make better?

Will: Lots of things. Taking clear pictures. Following the paths of cells. Checking mistakes. And helping other scientists find and use the maps. No one team can do every part alone.

Julian: Why not just buy a bigger microscope?

Will: A better microscope can help. But imagine taking a thousand photos of a giant puzzle. You still need to put the pieces together, check them, and work out what the picture shows.

Julian: So more pictures can also mean more work afterward.

Will: Exactly. The steps have to work together. A faster camera doesn't solve a problem in the next step. The program supports teams working on different parts of the job.

Julian: Does every team make the same kind of map?

Will: No. Some look at tiny connections between cells. Others follow longer paths between brain areas. Like a street map and a map of a building, each shows a different level of detail.

Julian: You pick the map that helps with your question.

Will: Yes. And learning how the different maps fit together is part of the work. You don't need the same view for every question.

Julian: What have scientists actually managed to map so far?

Will: Across the wider field, scientists have mapped a tiny worm's nervous system and an adult fruit fly brain. They've also made very detailed maps of small pieces of mouse and human brain.

Julian: Were all those maps made by BRAIN CONNECTS?

Will: No. Many teams and funding groups contributed to that longer story. BRAIN CONNECTS is part of the effort to move the science forward. The Neuronauts story explains some of those earlier steps.

Julian: Why use a fly if we want to understand people?

Will: Smaller brains let us study a whole network of cells and test our tools. That can teach us useful things. But a fly is different from a person, so we must check what carries over.

Julian: How can I tell whether a big project is making progress?

Will: Ask what changed. Did the team make a better tool? A larger map? Find and fix more mistakes? Or share data that other people can use? Those are real steps forward.

Julian: Not just, is the whole thing done yet?

Will: Right. And when you read an update, ask whether it describes a plan, a tool, or a finished result. Those can all matter, but they mean different things.

Julian: And the reason for all this work is to understand brains better.

Will: Yes. How we see, move, and learn, and what changes with illness or injury. That knowledge may help people in the future. It takes more than making a map to turn a discovery into care.

Julian: Let me try the short version. Connectomics is the science. BRAIN CONNECTS helps teams build the tools and maps to do that science.

Will: That's it. Next time, we'll follow one small piece of brain through the steps of making a map. You can follow along at NeuroTrailblazers dot org.

Julian: From a little piece to a big discovery. See you next time!

How do you make a brain map?

Follow a piece of preserved brain tissue through preparation, imaging, computer tracing, and human checking.

Download episode 3 (MP3, 4.7 MB)Download script
Read episode 3 script

Will: Welcome to the NeuroTrailblazers Podcast. I'm Will.

Julian: And I'm Julian. I'm six and a half. Dad, how does a piece of brain turn into one of those colorful maps?

Will: Let's walk through it. We'll follow the microscope method in the Neuronauts story. First we prepare a small piece of brain tissue so we can study its tiny parts.

Julian: Are you taking pictures inside somebody's head?

Will: For this method, no. The tissue has been removed and is no longer living. This is different from the kind of brain scan a person might have at a hospital.

Julian: Okay. What do you do with the little piece?

Will: Special chemicals help keep its shape. Other chemicals make the edges of the cells easier to see. Then we put it in a firm material that holds it steady.

Julian: Why do you need to see the edges?

Will: So we can tell which part belongs to which cell. Imagine trying to color a picture when you can't see the lines. You might accidentally color two different things as one.

Julian: I have done that. So now the lines are easier to see. What's next?

Will: We need to see inside. One way is to cut the piece into very thin slices, much thinner than a hair. A special cutting tool can have a diamond edge.

Julian: A real diamond? That's a fancy little knife.

Will: A very sharp one. The slices must stay in order. Some methods keep the slices, and others remove one thin layer at a time as they take pictures.

Julian: Why does the order matter so much?

Will: Think about a loaf of raisin bread. Look at the slices one by one, in order. The pictures help you work out where things were inside the whole loaf.

Julian: But we're looking for cell branches instead of raisins. And we can't mix up the slices.

Will: Exactly. A powerful microscope takes the pictures. It's called an electron microscope. It can show tiny details that an ordinary camera can't see.

Julian: My phone can zoom in. Could I just keep zooming?

Will: Zooming makes what's already in a picture bigger. It can't bring back a detail the camera never captured. We need a tool that can see those details in the first place.

Julian: Okay. Special pictures. Lots of slices. Are we at the map yet?

Will: Almost, but there are a few important steps left. First, a computer lines the pictures up. A slice may have moved or bent a little, so matching the pictures takes care.

Julian: Like straightening a stack of papers so the corners line up.

Will: That's the idea. Then we follow each cell from one picture to the next. Computers help label which pieces belong together. Each cell can get its own color on the screen.

Julian: Oh, so the cells aren't really purple and bright green?

Will: Those colors are added to help us see the map. We also look for the tiny places where cells connect. Following the branches tells us where they go. Finding connections tells us who connects to whom.

Julian: Can the computer get those paths wrong?

Will: Yes. It might join two different cells together. Or it might lose track of a branch and leave part out. People check the map against the original pictures and fix mistakes.

Julian: Like when I check my writing and find a word I missed.

Will: Right. This checking is called proofreading. Here you're checking cell shapes and connections. If you're unsure, you look more closely or ask someone else to help.

Julian: Then what do you do with the checked map?

Will: Use it to ask questions. For example, which cells connect to a group that helps an animal see movement? The map gives us clues we can test with other experiments.

Julian: So the point isn't just to make a really big, colorful picture.

Will: Exactly. The point is to learn something. Let's pause and see how much you remember. What comes before we can follow the paths?

Julian: Prepare the little piece. Make thin layers. Take pictures. Put the pictures in order. Then follow the cells and check their connections.

Will: You've got the journey. No need to remember every tool's name. And every step has people helping it work. Next time, we'll talk about ways you can get involved.

Julian: Even if you're still learning. Find the story at NeuroTrailblazers dot org. See you next time!

How can I get involved?

Small, concrete starting points for children, school and university students, and adults following the science.

Download episode 4 (MP3, 4.5 MB)Download script
Read episode 4 script

Will: Welcome to the NeuroTrailblazers Podcast. I'm Will.

Julian: I'm Julian, and I'm six and a half. Dad, what can I actually do with all this brain science?

Will: Let's make a plan for you, for older students, and for grown-ups who want to follow along. You don't have to become a scientist to enjoy learning how brains work.

Julian: Start with me. What could we do today?

Will: Open the Junior Lab at NeuroTrailblazers dot org together. Pick one short science story. Then draw a brain cell with its branches, or make one with pipe cleaners.

Julian: Can I make the branches different colors?

Will: Absolutely. The colors can help explain your model. Then tell someone what a branch does. Finish with one thing you learned and one question you still have.

Julian: I could bring the drawing to school. What could my class do?

Will: Ask your teacher about a short brain-map activity. Try drawing a map of the classroom first. Which places are connected? What can the map tell you, and what does it leave out?

Julian: Like it can't show who's sitting in every chair right now.

Will: Right. Then look at a brain map together and ask the same questions. Teachers can find lesson ideas and session materials on NeuroTrailblazers. You can learn a lot from one picture and a good question.

Julian: What about a student who's older than me?

Will: Here's a small project. Choose one neuron in a public brain viewer. Draw its shape, note where you found it, and write three things you notice. Then compare it with a second neuron.

Julian: Where would they find a viewer like that?

Will: FlyWire Codex lets people explore real fly brain maps. It has examples and lessons to help you start. A teacher or parent can help with the sign-in and the first few steps.

Julian: And the student could ask why the two cells look different.

Will: Yes. Label that as a question, not an answer you already know. Turn your observations into a short class presentation. Include the source so someone else can look at the same cells.

Julian: What if they want to help a real research team?

Will: Start with a practice lesson, such as checking a cell tracing. Then ask a teacher or mentor about supervised projects, local labs, or summer programs. Check who can apply and what training they need.

Julian: And what about someone at college?

Will: Pick a lab whose work interests you and read one of its project summaries. Write a short message saying what you found interesting, what you can already do, and what you'd like to learn.

Julian: Could they say, is there a small project I could help with?

Will: Yes. Ask about a supervised beginner task or a student placement. You might help check images, write a small computer program, or explain a result. What matters is matching a real need and learning to do it carefully.

Julian: What if a grown-up just wants to keep up with the discoveries?

Will: That's a good way to join in, too. Bookmark the NIH BRAIN Initiative news pages and the BRAIN CONNECTS website. Once a month, choose one update to read. You don't have to follow everything.

Julian: What should they look for in the update?

Will: Three things. What did the team actually do? Why does it matter? And what is still unknown? Check the date, too. A plan from last year isn't the same as a result announced today.

Julian: Could they listen to a talk instead of reading a big paper?

Will: Yes. Look for a public talk or a recorded research talk with an introduction. Pause when you need to. NeuroTrailblazers also has learning materials to help explain unfamiliar ideas.

Julian: And then tell somebody else what they learned?

Will: Try a short explanation: scientists map how brain cells connect so they can study how those cells work together. Say what the discovery shows, and don't turn a hopeful goal into a promise.

Julian: I like having something small to start with. Not a whole giant list of homework.

Will: Then choose just one thing this week. Kids: read and draw. Students: explore a map and make something you can show. Adults: follow one research update and ask a good question.

Julian: I'm picking a drawing and a question. You can find the starting links in our notes and at NeuroTrailblazers dot org. See you next time!

Choose one small next step

  • Kids and families: Read a story in the Junior Lab, draw a neuron, and bring one question to school.
  • School students: With a teacher or parent, explore two neurons in FlyWire Codex. Make a short presentation with three observations, one question, and a link to your source.
  • University students: Choose a lab, read a project summary, and ask about a supervised beginner project or student placement. Describe your interests and what you would like to learn.
  • Adults following the science: Pick one update each month from the NIH BRAIN Initiative or BRAIN CONNECTS. Ask what was done, why it matters, and what is still unknown.

The story behind the conversations

The Neuronauts Expedition provides the longer story and scientific references. For the program’s goals, see NIH’s BRAIN CONNECTS overview. Connectomics is the science of mapping neural connections; BRAIN CONNECTS is a research program helping develop the tools and capacity to make those maps. A map alone does not show all brain activity or explain every behavior.
