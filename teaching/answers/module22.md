This page answers every section of the worksheet for Module 22: Scientific
Presentation, in order. The worksheet lets learners present their own result or the
module's synthetic one. The responses
below are one **invented learner's** work on the synthetic result, written to show the
level of the kit's **Strong** criteria. The learner (Nadia), her lab, the volume and every
person who asks a question are invented. The scenario's figures, 3.2x, 847 connections
and about a third of arbors proofread, come from the module page and describe no real
dataset. Every further figure on this page (resolution, exclusion radius, rehearsal
times, rewiring count) is invented for the exemplar and is marked as such where it first
appears. This page is public and suitable for formative assessment.

The exemplar deliberately does not repeat the module's worked example (Maya's
reciprocity opener and her merge-error answer). A key that copied it would reward copying.

## Before you start

The prerequisites are figure familiarity and having presented one technical result. An
acceptable question to bring: “How much method do I have to show in a short talk?” The
module's talk-format decision table answers it; for this scenario, a short talk to a
room that includes segmentation specialists, the row is “contributed talk,” so the null
model and the reconstruction state go on a slide.

## Questions this module answers

1. **How do we simplify without distorting?** Change vocabulary, analogy and background
   depth; never what was measured, on which data, at which version, and what the result
   does not establish. The check is to write the caveat once for the segmentation
   specialists and once for the physiologist and confirm the two say the same thing.
2. **Which uncertainties must be explicit in oral presentation?** The rung on the
   uncertainty ladder, spoken; the reconstruction state (here, two thirds of the relevant
   arbors unproofread); and the dataset version. A listener cannot re-read a talk, so a
   caveat that is only on the slide was not delivered.
3. **What hidden expectations shape audience judgments?** The first minute is question,
   stake and news, not field history. In a connectomics room the reconstruction state is
   challenged first, so it belongs on a slide. “I don't know” paired with a next test
   reads as calibration; alone, it ends the conversation. Answer to the level asked, then
   stop. The chair owns the clock.

## The task

### 1. The four-slide mini-talk

**The claim tree, drawn before any slide.** Root question: do layer 4 excitatory cells
make more synapses onto PV+ interneurons than their connection counts alone predict?

- **Claim A.** L4 excitatory to PV+ synapses are 3.2x the degree-preserving null.
  Evidence: 847 connections at materialization T22 [invented version label], observed
  count against 1,000 rewirings [invented count]. Caveat: one null model; nothing yet
  controls for proximity.
- **Claim B.** The excess is not an artifact of unproofread arbors. Evidence: none yet.
  **Deleted.** Nadia has not rerun the count on the proofread subset, so this is a plan,
  not a claim. It moves to the limitation slide as the next test.
- **Claim C.** The result is anatomical. It says nothing about activity or about which
  way signals flow. Evidence: the measurement is a synapse count. Caveat: none needed;
  this is a boundary.

Deleting Claim B is the step the rubric rewards. A draft that kept it would have had a
slide with no evidence node under it.

**Time budget for the 3-minute slot:** 30 s problem, 45 s method, 60 s result, 30 s
limitation, 15 s spare. Four slides, one per node of the tree that survived.

**Slide 1, problem.** Headline: *Do L4 excitatory cells wire onto PV+ interneurons more
than their connection counts predict?* Body: one sentence of stake, “feedforward
inhibition models assume L4 recruits PV cells; an EM volume lets us count those synapses
one by one.” No figure. Spoken: the question, the stake, and “we counted, and we find
more than a shuffled version of the same wiring predicts.”

**Slide 2, method, with the provenance line.** Headline: *We counted synapses from L4
excitatory cells onto PV+ cells and compared the count with a degree-preserving null.*
Provenance line, verbatim on the slide [every element invented]: “Fictional cortical
volume; serial-section EM at 8 nm in plane; automated segmentation; materialization
T22, cell-type table at the same version; excluded: cells with somata within 50 µm of a
volume face. Proofread: about 1 in 3 of the L4 axons and PV+ dendrites involved.” Body:
a two-line description of the null in plain words, “keep every cell's number of inputs
and outputs, shuffle who connects to whom, count again, 1,000 times.” For the
physiologist, one spoken analogy: “like reseating the same guests at the same table
sizes and counting how often two particular people end up together.”

**Slide 3, result, with the rung named.** Headline: *L4 excitatory to PV+ synapses are
3.2x the degree-preserving null (847 connections, T22).* Body: the observed count
against the null distribution, the ratio, and the null's interval; a one-line note “n =
847 connections; not weighted by synapse count.” The rung is on the slide in words: **“This
suggests a wiring preference: it has survived one null model and no sensitivity
analysis.”** Spoken for the physiologist: “this is anatomy; it does not show that L4
drives these cells.”

**Slide 4, limitation and next test.** Headline: *Two thirds of the relevant arbors are
unproofread, and the null does not control for proximity.* Body, two lines: “Merge
errors add connections between cells that are already near each other, which is the
direction of this result, so 3.2x is an upper bound until the proofread subset is
rerun.” “Next: rerun on the proofread subset; add a degree-and-distance null.” Spoken
last sentence: “What this does not show is activity, direction of signal flow, or
anything about another volume.”

**Speaker notes with claim boundaries** (one line per slide, read exactly as written):

| Slide | Spoken claim | Boundary, also spoken |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | We asked whether L4 cells wire onto PV+ cells more than chance. | “Chance” here means a stated null, not a coin flip. |
| 2 | We counted at one version and compared with a degree-preserving null. | The null keeps degree only. Distance is not controlled. |
| 3 | The count is 3.2x the null. | Rung: “suggests.” One null, no sensitivity run, two thirds unproofread. |
| 4 | The bias from unproofread arbors points toward this result. | Nothing here is about activity or flow. |

The provenance line sits on the method slide, the rung on the result slide, and the same
rung (“suggests”) appears in the notes and in both prepared answers below.

### 2. Delivery against the timer

Rehearsal log [all times invented]:

| Run | Time | What changed before the next run |
|---|---|---|
| 1, alone, timer visible | 3:41 | Cut the sentence of field history that opened slide 1 (−18 s). Cut the second analogy on slide 2 (−14 s). Moved the null's rewiring count from speech to the slide (−9 s). |
| 2, alone, transitions spoken aloud | 3:02 | Wrote the slide 2 to 3 transition verbatim, because the improvised version was “so, um, the results”: *“That is what we counted and what we compared it with. Here is what came out.”* |
| 3, in front of two peers | 2:56 | Nothing cut. Slide 4 was delivered in full, which is the point of the budget. |

The learner did not speak faster between runs. Each cut is a scoping decision recorded
in the log, which is what the rubric means by “cut to the budget.” A run that fits the
budget with slide 4 delivered fully outranks one that fits by racing through the
limitation.

### 3. Two audience questions, typed before they are answered

Both were written before the talk, as the working checklist asks, and both were asked
in some form. The questioners are invented.

**Question 1, from a segmentation specialist (Kwame):** “Two thirds of your arbors are
unproofread. Isn't most of that 3.2x merge errors?”

Named aloud: *methods challenge*. The answer shape is mechanism, whether it was
checked, and the result or the plan.

> “The mechanism is real, and it points my way: a merge joins two arbors that are
> already close, so it adds L4-to-PV connections rather than removing them. I have not
> yet checked it; the proofread third is enough cells to rerun the count on, and I have
> not done that run. Until I have, I am treating 3.2x as an upper bound, and the rerun
> on the proofread subset is the next test.”

Three sentences, the rung unchanged (“suggests” on the slide; “upper bound until”
here), and it ends in the test. It does not claim the check was done, because it was
not. The module's own worked example has Maya answering a similar question with two
pieces of counter-evidence; Nadia has none yet, and saying so is the correct answer for
her state of work.

**Question 2, from the physiologist (Ingrid):** “Could this just be that L4 axons and
PV dendrites run through the same space, so they touch a lot? Proximity rather than
preference?”

Named aloud: *alternative explanation*. The answer shape is whether the null already
controls for it; if not, concede and name the test.

> “The null I used does not control for that. It keeps each cell's number of
> connections and shuffles the partners, so two cells that overlap heavily are as likely
> to be paired as two that never touch. If proximity is doing the work, a null that also
> keeps the distance between partners would shrink the 3.2x, and the site's worked
> reciprocity example shows exactly that kind of collapse. So the claim today is ‘more
> than degree predicts,’ not ‘more than proximity predicts,’ and the distance null is
> the test that separates them.”

Concedes the valid part (the null does not cover it), keeps the supported part (the
count exceeds the degree null), and ends in a test. It is one sentence longer than the
brief asks; the fourth sentence carries the boundary and is the one to keep if cutting.

**A third, for the taxonomy.** Ingrid also asks, “What is a materialization version?”
Named: *clarification*. One sentence: “A frozen snapshot of the segmentation and its
tables, so the count I showed can be rerun on exactly the same data.” Then stop.

**Weak versions, for contrast.**

- *Defensive, to Q1:* “The segmentation pipeline is state of the art and merge rates
  are low.” Asserts authority, quotes no number, names no mechanism, and invites “how
  low?”, which she cannot answer.
- *Capitulating, to Q1:* “That's fair, it's probably mostly artifact.” Abandons a
  result she has evidence for under mild challenge. The rubric flags this as a
  calibration failure, not modesty.
- *Over-answering, to the clarification:* a 90-second explanation of chunked graphs.
  Correct content, wrong length, and it costs the next questioner their turn.

### 4. Revision log

The peer critique ran under the one narrow brief: mark every sentence where the spoken
claim is stronger than the slide's evidence. Two marks came back, and each produced one
entry.

| What changed | From | To | Why |
|---|---|---|---|
| Slide 3 headline | “L4 excitatory neurons preferentially target PV+ interneurons” | “L4 excitatory to PV+ synapses are 3.2x the degree-preserving null (847 connections, T22)” | “Preferentially target” states a mechanism; the slide shows a count against one null. The headline now names the number, the null and the version, and the rung moved into the body text. |
| Spoken claim, slide 1 close | “L4 drives these interneurons.” | “L4 cells make more synapses onto PV+ cells than degree predicts; whether they drive them is a physiology question.” | A peer marked “drives” as an activity claim the anatomy cannot carry. The replacement keeps the finding and hands the functional question to the audience member who can test it. |

Two further marks were considered and not acted on, and the log says so: “roughly a
third proofread” was left as a fraction rather than a percentage because the exact
number differs by arbor class and a single percentage would overstate precision; the
analogy on slide 2 was kept because the physiologist said it was the sentence that
made the null make sense.

### What good looks like

- **Strong:** the deleted claim is visible in the tree; the provenance line names
  species-and-region, modality and resolution, pipeline, version and an exclusion
  rule; the rung is written on the result slide and repeated unchanged in the notes and
  both answers; each answer ends in a test; the revision log records a change that was
  refused and why.
- **Weak:** a slide deck with a title slide and no problem slide; a method slide that
  says “EM connectomics dataset” with no version; a result headline with a verb like
  “targets,” “prefers” or “drives”; a rung that appears only in speech; answers that
  end in a defense of the pipeline; a revision log that lists slide cosmetics.

## Evidence and reasoning

| # | Claim | Evidence | Limitation / what would change my mind |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | L4 excitatory to PV+ synapses exceed the degree-preserving null. | The scenario's count: 847 connections, 3.2x the null at T22. | One null. A degree-and-distance null that brought the ratio near 1 would make this a proximity result. |
| 2 | The reconstruction bias points toward the result. | Merges add connections between nearby arbors; two thirds of the relevant arbors are unproofread. | A rerun on the proofread third that kept the ratio would shrink this concern; one that halved it would make 3.2x a reconstruction number. |
| 3 | The talk's claim is anatomical only. | The measurement is a synapse count; no activity or perturbation data are in the volume. | None. This is a boundary, not a finding, and no observation would extend it. |

**Confidence:** Medium. One strong line of evidence (the count against one null), and
the two checks that would make it independent, the proofread subset and the distance
null, have not been run.

**Alternative considered and rejected:** opening with the rotating 3D reconstruction of
one L4 cell and its PV+ partners. Rejected because it holds the room for the exact
sixty seconds in which the question and the news should be stated, and because the
render shows a cell whose arbor is in the unproofread two thirds.

## Misconception self-check

Accept a response that names where the learner nearly made an error. Feedback for each:

- **A compelling narrative arc can carry a claim the evidence does not support.** Ask:
  “Which node of your claim tree is slide 3?” If the answer is a story beat rather than
  a node, the arc is carrying it. Nadia's Claim B was that beat; she deleted it.
- **Caveats can be dropped for a general audience because they would not understand
  them anyway.** Ask the learner to say the proofreading caveat to the physiologist in
  one sentence. “Two thirds of these wires have not been checked by a person yet” is
  understandable and logically identical to the specialist version.
- **Answering confidently is what makes an answer credible.** Compare the defensive
  answer to Q1 with the one that admits the check is not done. The second names a
  mechanism, a state of work and a test; the first names a feeling.
- **Speaking faster is a legitimate way to fit more material into a fixed slot.**
  Point to the rehearsal log: 41 seconds came out as three cuts, not as pace.
- **Hedging weakens a talk and should be minimized.** The rung word “suggests” is what
  lets the audience believe the 3.2x. A talk with no rungs has one rung, and the
  audience picks which.
- **Every question in Q&A is an attack that must be rebutted.** Ingrid's proximity
  question handed Nadia her next analysis. Naming the type first (alternative
  explanation) is what stops the reflex to defend.

## Session timing (facilitator reference)

This section has no learner task. For the 00:00–08:00 exemplar, two openers for the
synthetic result, side by side:

- *Field history first:* “Connectomics has transformed how we study cortical circuits.
  Electron microscopy now lets us reconstruct every synapse in a volume of tissue.
  Today I'll talk about inhibitory connectivity in layer 4.” Thirty seconds spent; no
  question, no stake, no news.
- *Question first:* “Do layer 4 excitatory cells make more synapses onto PV+
  interneurons than their connection counts predict? Feedforward inhibition models
  assume they do, and an EM volume lets us count. We counted, and we find 3.2 times
  what a shuffled version of the same wiring predicts, with a caveat about proofreading
  I will come to.” The same thirty seconds, and the audience knows how hard to listen.

For the 42:00–54:00 simulation, assign Q1 and Q2 above as the methods-challenge and
alternative-explanation questions if a learner is using the synthetic result, and have
the room name the type before judging the answer.

## Rubric

A self-assessment that matches this exemplar:

- **Strongest part:** the rung is the same word on the slide, in the notes and in both
  answers, and the deleted claim is visible in the tree with the reason it was deleted.
- **Weakest part:** both prepared answers end in tests that have not been run, so the
  talk's confirmatory path is entirely future work. **Next action:** run the count on
  the proofread subset before the next delivery, so at least one answer can end in a
  result rather than a plan.

## Exit prompt

The 60-second opener, 132 words, timed at 55 seconds in rehearsal [invented timing]:

> Do layer 4 excitatory cells wire onto PV+ interneurons more than their connection
> counts alone predict? Feedforward inhibition models assume they do, and an EM volume
> lets us count those synapses one at a time instead of inferring them. We counted, at
> one frozen version of a reconstructed cortical volume, and we find 3.2 times as many
> layer 4 to PV+ synapses as a null model that keeps every cell's number of connections
> and shuffles the partners. One caveat before the details: about a third of the
> arbors involved have been proofread, and the errors in the other two thirds push in
> the direction of this result, so treat 3.2 as an upper bound until I have rerun it on
> the checked cells. If you are already thinking “proximity, not preference,” hold that
> question: the null does not control for it, and it is the next test.

The four required parts: the question (sentence 1), the evidence-backed finding
(sentence 3, with the version and the null named), the explicit caveat (sentence 4),
and the pre-emption of the expected challenge (sentence 5). If it ran long, the
background sentence to cut first is the second one.

## Peer review (swap worksheets)

A reviewer of this exemplar should check that every spoken claim in the notes table
has a boundary beside it and that both answers end in a test. A good question to ask
its author: “You call 3.2x an upper bound. What number, from the proofread subset,
would make you drop the word ‘suggests’ for ‘consistent with’?”

## Feedback guide

This key follows the kit's own tiers rather than a numeric score.

- **Minimum pass:** every slide's claim has an evidence item and its caveat is visible;
  the physiologist could follow the method without the claim changing; each Q&A answer
  names a limit and a follow-up test; the method slide names the dataset and version.
- **Strong performance:** the most likely critique is pre-empted in the opener and on
  slide 4; the null is explained in plain words without becoming a coin flip; the
  answer to Q2 concedes what the null does not cover while keeping the count; the rung
  is identical in slide, speech and both answers.
- **Common failures to flag:** a method slide compressed to “EM connectomics dataset,”
  which is how “preferentially target” gets onto the result slide; a jargon opener that
  loses the physiologist by sentence two; an answer to Q1 that cites the pipeline's
  reputation; and over-conceding, “it's probably an artifact,” which abandons a
  supported count under mild challenge.

Do not reward slide polish, the number of backup slides or a confident delivery of an
unbounded claim. Do not penalize a learner whose answer to Q1 is “I have not run that
yet, and it is next”; that is the correct answer for work at this stage. Do not reward
a rehearsal log that fits the budget by pace. Accept a different rung for the result if
the reason is stated: a learner who calls 3.2x “consistent with a wiring preference”
because the proximity alternative is untested has made a defensible call. This is a
local teaching rubric, not a validated assessment instrument.
