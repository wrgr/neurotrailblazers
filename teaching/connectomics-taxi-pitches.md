# Explain connectomics on a taxi ride

“What do you work on?” You have a few sentences, no slides, and someone who may have other things on their mind. Start a conversation they can join.

[The Neuronauts Expedition]({{ '/neuronauts/' | relative_url }}) provides the full story: why map the brain, how the work happens, what has been achieved, and what comes next. These samples help you tell a small, accurate part of that story aloud. **Science communication means choosing what helps this listener, while keeping the evidence intact.**

## Before you start

**Time:** 10 minutes. **Prerequisites:** none; read Neuronauts for the scientific background. **You need:** a timer and, if possible, a partner. **You finish with:** a short explanation in your own words and an answer to one follow-up question. The times below are approximate speaking times, not deadlines to race through.

You will practice explaining the purpose of brain mapping, distinguishing a result from a goal, and answering a question without overstating the science. Adapt “I” and “we” to your actual role; the samples do not confer a project affiliation.

## 1. Begin with a question the listener recognizes

### About 15 seconds: “What do you do?”

> I work on maps of how brain cells connect. Those connections help us see, move, and learn. We want to understand how the parts work together, and what happens when something goes wrong.

Then pause. The listener may ask about the pictures, the diseases, your job, or nothing at all. A short answer is a complete answer if they want to return to their day.

### About 30 seconds: “What kind of brain map?”

> A brain scan can show large structures, but it doesn't show every tiny connection between brain cells. We use detailed images and computers to trace those connections. It's a bit like moving from a map of a city's neighborhoods to a map of its individual streets. The aim is to understand how brain circuits work. A wiring map is one piece of the evidence; we also need to study what the cells actually do.

### About 60 seconds: “Why would anybody need that?”

> Your brain lets you recognize a friend, catch a ball, and learn a new skill. Those abilities depend on cells working together. We know a lot about the cells, but we're still learning how their connections support those abilities.
>
> Connectomics is the effort to map those connections. For very detailed maps, researchers take microscope pictures, use computers to trace the cells, and check the results for mistakes. Maps have already helped explain how circuits in the eye detect the direction of movement.
>
> The bigger hope is that understanding circuits will help us understand brain disorders and develop better ways to help people. That takes more than a map: it takes experiments to find out what a circuit does and whether changing it helps. My part is **[one concrete sentence about your actual work]**.

**Evidence:** retinal circuit findings are established; the broader medical benefit is a research aim. See sources below. Replace the bracketed sentence before using this pitch.

## 2. Explain the program without a list of acronyms

### “What is BRAIN CONNECTS?”

> It's a research program supported by the National Institutes of Health. Teams are building better ways to map connections across brains, from large pathways down to tiny connections between cells. They develop tools, make maps, and share data so researchers can investigate how circuits work. It's a coordinated effort across many projects, not one team making one picture.

If asked, the name expands to **BRAIN Initiative Connectivity Across Scales**. Lead with what it does; save the expanded name for someone who wants it.

### “Where are you now?”

> The field has mapped a tiny worm's nervous system, an adult fruit fly brain, and small pieces of mouse and human brain in extraordinary detail. Larger maps are still a major challenge. BRAIN CONNECTS is developing the methods and research capacity to go further. Those earlier achievements came from different projects; they weren't all produced by CONNECTS.

For a specific project's status, add a **dated, verified result**, such as a released dataset. “Targets ten cubic millimeters” describes a goal; it does not tell someone how much has been imaged, reconstructed, checked, or released. If you cannot verify the current stage, say, “I can explain the goal, but I'd need to check the team's latest update for its progress.”

### “What is NeuroTrailblazers?”

> NeuroTrailblazers helps people learn how to build and use detailed brain maps. The science needs people who can work with the images, check computer-generated reconstructions, and ask good questions. We turn those skills into lessons and practice activities that people can use openly.

## 3. Let their question choose the next sentence

**“Can you read my thoughts?”**

> Not from a wiring map alone. It shows connections, not everything those cells are doing right now. Think of a road map: it doesn't tell you where every driver is going.

**“Will this cure Alzheimer's?”**

> It may help researchers understand circuits involved in memory and how they change. But a map is not a treatment, and finding a difference doesn't prove what caused an illness. That needs further experiments and clinical evidence.

**“Why use mice if you want to help people?”**

> Mice let researchers study mammalian circuits and test methods in ways that aren't possible in people. We still have to test which findings apply to humans. Human tissue and human imaging are also important.

**“Can't AI do it?”**

> Artificial intelligence does much of the tracing, but it can accidentally join two cells or break one into pieces. Those errors can change what the map says. We need ways to measure reliability, including checks by trained people.

**“Why spend money on a map instead of a treatment?”**

> We need both. A better map can help researchers choose more precise questions and experiments. It is research infrastructure that many teams can reuse, but its value still has to be shown through useful science and, eventually, benefits to people.

## 4. Watch an explanation become more accurate

**First attempt:** “We're mapping the human brain so we can cure Alzheimer's.”

**What I would change:** That sentence collapses three different claims: what tissue is being studied, whether its map is finished, and whether a treatment has been demonstrated. I would first check the actual project and its current result. Without those facts, I would describe the field's purpose rather than claim a specific achievement.

**Revised:** “We study how brain cells connect, including circuits involved in memory. Detailed maps can help us test ideas about how those circuits work. We hope that knowledge will eventually help people with brain disorders, but that link needs further evidence.”

**What would change the wording?** A verified dataset release would let me name a completed map. A demonstrated circuit finding would let me explain what was learned. A medical claim would need evidence of patient benefit, not just an interesting image.

## 5. Practice a conversation, not a recital

1. Write a 30-second answer to “What do you work on?” Include a familiar purpose, a concrete action, and why it matters.
2. Read it aloud and time it. Replace one unexplained technical term. Remove a sentence if you need to rush.
3. Ask a partner which part they would ask about next. Answer that question in two sentences, then stop.
4. Ask them to explain back what is already possible and what remains a goal. Revise anything that gave the wrong impression.

**Artifact:** your pitch, their follow-up question, your answer, and one supporting source.

| Level | What the listener can take away |
|---|---|
| Not yet | Technical terms or unsupported promises obscure what the work does. |
| Proficient | The listener can explain the purpose and distinguish an achievement from a hope. |
| Strong | The explanation responds to the listener's interest, includes a concrete supported example, and preserves an important limit without becoming a lecture. |

## Recover when an explanation misses

- **You gave a string of project names:** return to one sentence about the question being studied.
- **An analogy became too literal:** name its limit. Neurons are living cells, not passive electrical cables.
- **You promised an outcome:** distinguish the demonstrated result from the hoped-for benefit.
- **You lost the listener:** stop adding detail. Ask which part, if any, they would like to hear more about.

## Scope and sources

These are sample conversations, not official program statements or a live project-progress dashboard. They do not establish a medical benefit or substitute for a project's latest report. They use the Neuronauts story as background and preserve the distinction between program goals, scientific results, and longer-term applications.
