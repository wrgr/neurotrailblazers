**Time:** 20 minutes in pairs, 15 minutes in pairs, then 15 minutes alone. The case
below is **invented**. Rin, Dr. Whitfield, Dr. Kessler and Kai are not real people.

## The case

Rin has just finished a scoped proofreading project in a connectomics lab. Dr.
Whitfield, the PI, says: "Pick a question you're excited about and tell me by the end
of the month." That leaves three weeks. Rin has three candidates:

- **A. Extend the lab's interneuron analysis to the new volume.** The pipeline exists.
  Kai, a senior student, says: "Just do A. It's the quickest paper."
- **B. Build a better automated synapse classifier.** Rin enjoys the machine learning.
  Dr. Kessler, a postdoc, is keen. The lab has no ground-truth labels for the new volume.
- **C. Test whether some axons make repeated contacts onto the same target more often
  than chance.** Rin found the idea in a journal club paper. Nobody in the lab has
  done this analysis. It may need more proofreading than the volume has.

Rin has spent two weeks reading about all three and feels less sure than at the start.
Rin has not asked anyone a direct question about any of them.

## 1. Cannot know yet, or have not asked? (20 minutes)

For each option, list:

- **Known now:** what Rin can already say about it.
- **Knowable in two weeks:** open questions a short piece of work or one conversation
  would answer. Name the work or the person.
- **Not knowable yet:** questions only months of work would answer.

Then answer: which option has the most uncertainty that could be removed in two
weeks? Why is that a reason to probe it first, rather than to choose it?

## 2. Design a reversible probe (15 minutes)

Design one probe for Rin. It must fit in two weeks. Write:

1. **The one question** it answers, in one sentence.
2. **What Rin will do**, day by day or in three steps.
3. **The artifact** it leaves behind even if Rin drops the option.
4. **The stopping rule**, written now: "If by ___ I see ___, I will ___; otherwise ___."
5. **The reversibility check:** how long it commits Rin, what stopping early costs,
   and what it makes easier next.

Then write the two sentences Rin sends Dr. Whitfield to propose it.

## 3. Your direction memo (15 minutes, private)

This section is yours. You do not have to share it. Pick a real choice you face. It
can be a research question, a method, a course, or which task comes first.

1. **The choice**, in one sentence, and the date you must decide by.
2. **Two or three options.** For each: known now, knowable in two weeks, not knowable yet.
3. **Whose preference is carrying the most weight** right now: yours, a supervisor's,
   a peer's, or a guess about what looks good? Write the name.
4. **Your probe**, with a stopping rule written in advance.
5. **What would show you chose wrong**, in six months, stated as something you could observe.
6. **Who you will show this memo to**, and the one question you will ask them.

## Peer check and exit ticket

Swap Rin's probe with your partner, not your memo. Your partner answers one question:
*on the stop date, could someone other than Rin tell whether the rule was met?* If
not, rewrite the stopping rule.

Exit ticket: "My probe is ___. It stops on ___ (date). I will show my memo to ___."

**Follow-through this week:** run the first week of your probe, or take the first step
your memo names. Show the memo to one mentor and write down one thing they would
change. Keep the memo; a later workshop asks for it.

**Success criteria:** unknowns are split into knowable-soon and not-yet; the probe has
one question, one artifact and a stopping rule set in advance; the proposal to Dr.
Whitfield can be approved in one reply; your memo names a decision date and an
observable sign of a wrong choice.
