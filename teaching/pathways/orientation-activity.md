**Time:** 25 minutes in pairs, then 20 minutes alone. The case below is **invented**.
Dana and Dr. Okafor are not real people, and any resemblance to your own lab is the point.

## The case

Dana joined a connectomics lab three weeks ago. Here is everything Dana has been told,
in order:

1. Day 1, from Dr. Okafor (the PI): "Get set up, read the lab wiki, and have a look at
   the proofreading backlog."
2. Day 3, lab manager: "Lab meeting is Thursdays. New people don't usually present."
3. Day 8, Dr. Okafor, in the corridor: "We should think about whether the axon
   classifier is worth retraining."
4. Day 10, a senior student: "Don't bother Okafor with small stuff. Ask me."
5. Day 15, email from Dr. Okafor, one line: "How's it going?"

Dana has done this:

- Read the wiki. Proofread 40 segments from the backlog, unevenly, because Dana was not
  sure what counted as finished.
- Said nothing about the classifier. Dana assumed it was a passing thought.
- Asked the senior student two questions. The student answered one.
- Replied to the day-15 email: "Going well, thanks!"

On day 21, Dr. Okafor asks in a one-to-one: "Where are we on the classifier question?"

## 1. Told versus inferred (10 minutes)

For each of the five things Dana was told, write:

- What was literally said.
- What Dana inferred it meant.
- One other plausible meaning.

Then answer: which inference did the most damage by day 21, and why that one?

## 2. Rewrite the ambiguity (15 minutes)

Pick **two** of the five statements. For each, write the single question Dana should
have asked within a day of hearing it. A good question:

- can be answered in one sentence;
- names a deliverable, a date, or a threshold;
- does not ask for reassurance.

Then write Dana's reply to the day-15 email again. It should report progress, name
one thing Dana is unsure about, and state a default action with a date.

## 3. Your own list (20 minutes, private)

This section is yours. You do not have to share it.

1. Write **five** things you believe your supervisor, or the person you most answer
   to, expects of you. Mark each **S** if it was stated or **I** if you inferred it.
2. For each **I**, write the evidence that led you to infer it.
3. Write a stuck-threshold question: how long you should be blocked before you ask,
   and who you ask.
4. Draft your check-in script. Start from this and make it yours:

> "I want to check I have your expectations right. Here is what I think you expect from
> me: [your list]. What is missing, and what have I got wrong?"

## Peer check and exit ticket

Swap only your script with your partner, not your list. Your partner answers one
question: *could a supervisor correct this, or does it only ask to be reassured?*
Revise one sentence.

Exit ticket: "The expectation I am least sure of is ___. I will check it by ___ (date)."

**Follow-through this week:** hold the check-in. Add one correction to your list.
Bring the corrected list to the next workshop.

**Success criteria:** told and inferred are kept separate; rewritten questions have a
deliverable, date or threshold; your list marks inferences honestly; your script
invites correction.
