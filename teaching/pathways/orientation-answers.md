These responses use the worksheet's **invented** case. They are examples, not the only
acceptable wording. This page is public and suitable for formative feedback.

## 1. Told versus inferred

| Said | Dana's inference | Another plausible meaning |
|---|---|---|
| "Have a look at the proofreading backlog." | Background familiarization; do some. | A deliverable: clear a defined portion, to a standard, by a date. |
| "New people don't usually present." | Stay quiet at lab meeting. | You won't present *yet*; attending and asking one question is still expected. |
| "We should think about whether the classifier is worth retraining." | A passing thought, no action. | A task: come back with a view and a recommendation. |
| "Ask me, not Okafor." | Route everything through the student. | Route small *technical* questions; decisions and updates still go to the PI. |
| "How's it going?" | Social pleasantry. | A request for a status report, from someone who cannot see progress unless told. |

**The most damaging inference** is the classifier one. The other four cost time or
visibility. That one cost a deliverable the PI thought had been assigned. It also
combines two items from the lab norms page at once: a suggestion made in passing is
still a suggestion, and silence is not consent. Accept the backlog as the answer if
the learner argues that forty unevenly finished segments misrepresent Dana's effort.

## 2. Rewritten questions

- **Backlog:** "For the backlog, what counts as done for one segment, and how many do
  you want finished by when?"
- **Classifier:** "On the classifier, do you want me to come back with a
  recommendation? If so, by when, and what would you want it to include?"
- **Lab meeting:** "Is there anything you'd like me to contribute at lab meeting before
  I present, for example one question per meeting?"
- **Senior student:** "Which kinds of question should go to you and which should go to
  Dr. Okafor?" (Ask the student, not the PI.)

**Rewritten day-15 reply:**

> "Going reasonably. I've read the wiki and proofread 40 backlog segments, though I'm
> not sure I'm applying the same standard of done to all of them. Could you or someone
> tell me what finished looks like? Also, on the classifier: unless you'd rather I
> didn't, I'll write half a page on whether retraining is worth it and send it by
> Friday."

The reply reports numbers, names one uncertainty and states a default with a date.
It turns day 21 from a surprise into a scheduled check.

## 3. Personal list: what good looks like

The private list is not collected, but learners who share it tend to fall into two
patterns worth naming aloud.

**Strong:** at least two items marked **I**, each with specific evidence ("I infer
weekly updates are expected because the last two lab members sent them"). The script
reads the list and asks what is wrong.

**Weak:** all five items marked **S**, or items so general they cannot be wrong
("work hard", "be professional"). The script asks "Am I doing OK?" That question asks
for reassurance and invites a one-word answer. It does not surface a gap.

## Feedback rubric

Score four dimensions **0–2 each**:

1. **Told and inferred kept separate.** 2 = every row has a literal quote and a
   distinct inference; 1 = most rows; 0 = the two are merged.
2. **Questions are answerable.** 2 = both rewritten questions name a deliverable, date
   or threshold; 1 = one does; 0 = both are open-ended.
3. **Status reply is useful.** 2 = progress, uncertainty and a dated default; 1 = two
   of the three; 0 = still "Going well".
4. **Check-in invites correction.** 2 = the script reads a specific list and asks what
   is wrong; 1 = specific list, reassurance-seeking ask; 0 = no list.

A proficient response scores at least 6/8 with no zero on dimension 4. This is a local
teaching rubric, not a validated assessment instrument.

Do not grade whether the learner's own expectations are *correct*. Only the
supervisor can tell them that, which is the point of the check-in.
