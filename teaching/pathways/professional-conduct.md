*Workshop 8 of the [Professional Pathways workshops]({{ '/teaching/pathways/' | relative_url }}).
90 minutes, no slides required.*

- [Learner worksheet]({{ '/teaching/pathways/professional-conduct-activity/' | relative_url }})
- [Instructor model responses]({{ '/teaching/pathways/professional-conduct-answers/' | relative_url }})
- Background: [Lab norms]({{ '/hidden-curriculum/lab-norms/' | relative_url }}), especially
  *Escalation: when something is wrong* and *When you have made a mistake that affects
  someone else's work*; [Conflict]({{ '/hidden-curriculum/conflict/' | relative_url }}#harassment-and-misconduct),
  especially *Harassment and misconduct*

**Question:** What are my obligations, and what do I do when I see them breached?

## Outcomes and preparation

Learners tell research misconduct apart from honest error and from a difference of
opinion; state their obligation in a situation as an action, not a feeling; choose the
lowest escalation rung that is adequate; and write a four-part error report about a
mistake of their own.

The working definition is the US federal one used by the Office of Research Integrity
([ORI](https://ori.hhs.gov/)): research misconduct is **fabrication, falsification or
plagiarism** in proposing, performing or reviewing research, or in reporting results.
It does not include honest error or differences of opinion. Institutions differ, and many
use broader local policies. Learners outside the US should check their own institution's
and funder's policy; say this at the start.

[Module 19]({{ '/modules/module19/' | relative_url }}) and its [session
kit]({{ '/teaching/sessions/module19/' | relative_url }}) put learners in the reviewer's
chair, auditing someone else's manuscript for integrity risks. This workshop is
first-person. It asks what *you* owe when you see a problem in your own lab, and what you
say when the error is yours. The rulings use the rungs from lab norms; the error report
uses its four-part script.

Before class, read both background sections and the worksheet scenarios. Find out whether
your role makes you a required reporter under your institution's policy, and write down
the names of your ombuds office and research integrity officer so you can give them out.
**The scenarios are invented.** Nobody is asked to describe a real incident.

## Timed plan and facilitator cues

- **0–10 min:** state your reporting status first: "If you tell me about a real incident,
  here is what I am required to do with it." Then tell a short story about an error of
  your own that you reported, and how long you waited before reporting it. Ask: "What
  made the wait feel reasonable at the time?"
- **10–20 min:** write three columns on the board: *misconduct (FFP)*, *honest error*,
  *difference of opinion*. Add a fourth, *conduct that is not FFP*, for harassment and
  bullying, which have their own routes. Then draw the four rungs from lab norms, 0 to 3.
- **20–50 min:** pairs rule on the five scenarios in worksheet sections 1 and 2. At
  minute 35, check that pairs are writing the obligation *before* the rung. Expect most
  pairs to over-escalate scenario 1 (a disagreement about boundary cells) and to
  under-escalate scenario 3 (the unchecked backlog).
- **50–60 min:** debrief. Take scenario 3 first. Ask: "What would Sam have to know before
  calling it falsification?" Then scenario 5. Point out that the first move at rung 3 is a
  question about confidentiality, asked before any details.
- **60–75 min:** individuals write Priya's four-part error report (section 3), then start
  their own rung map (section 4). The rung map is private writing.
- **75–85 min:** pairs swap *only the error report*. The partner checks one thing: does
  the first sentence say what happened, with no apology in front of it?
- **85–90 min:** exit ticket. Name the follow-through task and the date you will ask
  about it.

## Facilitator cautions

Scenario 5 involves conduct toward a person. It may be close to someone's experience.
Keep the discussion on the invented case. If a learner starts describing a real
incident, stop them gently before details: "I want to hear this, and I want you to know
first what I would be obliged to do with it. Can we talk after?" Then follow your
institution's policy.

Do not rule on real cases in the room, even hypothetically framed ones. Point to the
ombuds office, the research integrity officer, and a named program contact. Say that
this workshop teaches a procedure and does not replace those offices.

Do not present escalation as cost-free. Lab norms says plainly that the risk falls
unevenly, for example on someone whose visa is tied to their position. Let learners say
that the norm is unfair; it is on the page for that reason.

## Assessment and follow-through

Use the four-dimension rubric on the model responses page. Collect sections 1–3.
Section 4 stays with the learner.

**Follow-through:** complete the rung map by finding out, for your ombuds office and one
other rung-3 route, what they are required to report. Ask it as a procedural question,
with no incident attached. Start [STEM Identity and
Purpose]({{ '/teaching/pathways/identity-and-purpose/' | relative_url }}) by asking who
did it and what they learned.
